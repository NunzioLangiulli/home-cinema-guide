# Aspect ratios and black bars

> Main source: Slidebean, *The brilliant scam of cinematic "black bars"* (V4, 2026-07-07), [video](https://youtu.be/JYg0rBfAz6U), with contributions from Paul Carlin.

## In short

- TVs are **16:9 (1.78:1)**. Films can be 1.33, 1.37, 1.66, 1.85, 2.00, 2.20, 2.39, 2.76… Any ratio other than 16:9 produces **black bars**: that is **correct**, not a fault.
- **Don't** use zoom or "fill screen" on films: you crop the framing or distort the picture.
- 16:9 is itself a compromise designed to hold cinema ratios with small bars.

## How much of the screen the bars take

Formula: bars = 1 − (narrower ratio ÷ wider ratio).

| Film ratio | On a 16:9 TV | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1.33 (4:3) | 25 % (side bars) | Classic TV, *Zack Snyder's Justice League* |
| 1.37 (Academy) | 23 % (sides) | Classics 1932–1953 |
| 1.66 | 7 % (sides) | *The Brutalist* (VistaVision) |
| 1.85 | 4 % (top/bottom) | Most "flat" films |
| 2.00 (Univisium) | 11 % | *Stranger Things*, *House of the Dragon* |
| 2.20 | 19 % | Classic 70 mm (Super Panavision 70) |
| 2.39 | 26 % | Anamorphic / "scope" |
| 2.76 | 35 % | *The Hateful Eight* (Ultra Panavision 70) |

✅ The video's figures are correct ([V4 03:18](https://youtu.be/JYg0rBfAz6U?t=198), [V4 19:41](https://youtu.be/JYg0rBfAz6U?t=1181), [V4 26:17](https://youtu.be/JYg0rBfAz6U?t=1577)).

## A short history of aspect ratios

- ✅ 4-perf 35 mm: a ~1.33 frame (silent era). The optical soundtrack narrows the picture (~1.19, e.g. Movietone), then the Academy standardizes **1.37** in 1932 ([V4 07:46](https://youtu.be/JYg0rBfAz6U?t=466)).
- ✅ TV adopts **4:3 (1.33)**, very close to the cinema ratio of the time ([V4 09:25](https://youtu.be/JYg0rBfAz6U?t=565)).
- ✅ 1950s: TV spreads (in the US from 1–2 % of homes in 1946 to over half by 1954; the video's figures match historical data) and the *Paramount* decision (1948) separates studios from cinemas. Hollywood answers with **widescreen**, designed to offer what TV cannot ([V4 09:58](https://youtu.be/JYg0rBfAz6U?t=598)):
  - **Cinerama** (1952): three projectors, ~2.59, multichannel sound on separate magnetic film.
  - **CinemaScope** (Fox, 1953): anamorphic lenses, 2.55 → 2.35 → 2.39 (the SMPTE standard moved to 2.39 in 1970).
  - **VistaVision** (Paramount, 1954): horizontal 8-perf 35 mm, a negative twice as large, usually projected at 1.66 or 1.85. Later used for effects on *Star Wars* and other films, and recently for *The Brutalist* ([V4 15:54](https://youtu.be/JYg0rBfAz6U?t=954)).
  - **Todd-AO / Super Panavision 70**: 65 mm negative, 70 mm prints with 6-track magnetic sound, ~2.20.
  - **Ultra Panavision 70**: 65 mm **and** anamorphic (1.25×), **2.76**. A few films in the 1950s–60s, then *The Hateful Eight* (2015) ([V4 25:44](https://youtu.be/JYg0rBfAz6U?t=1544)).
  - ⚠️ "The widest format in history" is an overstatement: wider experimental formats exist (e.g. Polyvision for *Napoléon*, 1927, about 4:1).
- ✅ "Flat" 1.85 was often obtained by **matting** a full-frame negative (*open matte*). That is why some 4:3 VHS tapes show *more* picture top and bottom than in cinemas (*Titanic*, *The Shining*) ([V4 29:01](https://youtu.be/JYg0rBfAz6U?t=1741)). ⚠️ It was not always intended: microphones or set edges sometimes appear.
- ✅ 16:9 for TV was proposed in the 1980s (SMPTE, Kerns Powers) as the geometric mean between 4:3 and 2.35.

## Aspect ratio as an artistic choice

✅ Examples cited: *The Lighthouse* (Robert Eggers, 1.19:1) for claustrophobia; *Mommy* (Xavier Dolan, 1:1), where the protagonist physically "pushes open" the frame; Wes Anderson switching ratios ([V4 28:28](https://youtu.be/JYg0rBfAz6U?t=1708)).
💬 Paul Carlin: "it all depends on what the director wants" ([V4 16:26](https://youtu.be/JYg0rBfAz6U?t=986)).

## Streaming and bars: why Netflix doesn't fill the screen

Reasons given in the video ([V4 35:35](https://youtu.be/JYg0rBfAz6U?t=2135)):
1. ✅ Creative freedom: the service accepts the director's chosen ratio.
2. ✅ Visual effects: less area to render.
3. ⚠️ Bandwidth: black bars compress almost for free, leaving more bits for the picture. Real but small.
4. 💬 Psychology: bars "feel like cinema".

⚠️ The video presents the **2:1 "Univisium"** ratio as Netflix's. It was actually proposed by cinematographer **Vittorio Storaro** in the late 1990s as a single ratio for cinema and TV; Netflix and others adopted it later.

## IMAX and variable aspect ratios

- ✅ IMAX 15/70 (horizontal 70 mm film, 15 perforations), ~1.43:1 in the largest venues; IMAX digital 1.90:1.
- ✅ Films with IMAX sequences (*The Dark Knight*, *Interstellar*, *Oppenheimer*) have a **variable aspect ratio**: some Blu-ray/UHD releases keep it (1.78 in IMAX scenes, 2.39 elsewhere), many streaming versions don't ([V4 33:59](https://youtu.be/JYg0rBfAz6U?t=2039)).
- ✅ Disney+ offers "IMAX Enhanced" versions of some Marvel films with more vertical picture.
- 💬 Carlin raises a fair concern ([V4 35:04](https://youtu.be/JYg0rBfAz6U?t=2104)): a film framed for 2.39 and "opened up" for IMAX is not necessarily the director's preferred framing. Choose knowingly.
- ⚠️ "IMAX can't be edited digitally" ([V4 32:52](https://youtu.be/JYg0rBfAz6U?t=1972)): IMAX negatives are routinely scanned (8K for *Oppenheimer*, see [image pipeline](01-image-pipeline.md#imax-70-mm)); physical negative cutting is for the film prints.
